## 4. Source-by-source findings with exact pinpoints

### 4.1 Kramer (1956), printed pp. 307-310

**Q1 — named procedures the construction applies to.** The paper names Duncan, Keuls, Newman, and Tukey as writers who developed multiple range tests for equally replicated treatments (printed p. 307, second paragraph). It states that the extension is exemplified with reference to Duncan's New Multiple Range Test but is applicable to any of those writers' tests, the only requirement being use of their tabled ranges (printed p. 307). The construction is therefore presented as generic across the multiple-range family, with Duncan's test as the worked vehicle, **not** as a modification of one Studentized-range all-pairs procedure.

**Q2 — effective standard-error construction.** For equal replication, significance requires the difference of two ranked means to exceed a shortest significant range obtained by multiplying the standard error of a mean by a tabled significant Studentized range value, indexed by the error degrees of freedom and by the number of means spanned (printed p. 307). For unequal replication the paper's displayed criterion rewrites the comparison so that the mean difference is multiplied by the factor `sqrt(2 n_B n_C / (n_B + n_C))` and compared against a single factor `R' = s z_{p,n2}`, where `s^2` is the error mean square (printed p. 308). This is algebraically the average-of-reciprocals standard error `s sqrt((1/n_i + 1/n_j)/2)`, that is, the construction later called the Tukey-Kramer modification. The paper's own recommendation is that a table of such factors be set up for the individual case (printed p. 308).

**Q3 — the object proposed.** A **test**, specifically a grouping and significance-declaration rule for ranked means. The paper's closing display underscores means and states that any two means not underscored by the same line are significantly different in the sense defined (printed p. 310). It is not framed as a confidence procedure, and the words "simultaneous" and "confidence" do not occur anywhere in the article (Section 6 absence record).

**Q4 — assumptions.** Stated assumptions are minimal and largely **inherited** rather than declared. The paper works from an analysis-of-variance error mean square with its degrees of freedom (printed pp. 308, 309 numerical example) and from Duncan's tabled Studentized ranges (printed pp. 307, 309). The article states no explicit normality, independence, or homogeneity-of-variance condition of its own; those conditions come with the multiple-range framework it extends, whose sole cited source is Duncan (1955), the article's only reference (printed p. 310). One internal consistency check is given: for two means the criterion coincides with a t-test, from which the paper derives the relation between the tabled range value and Student's t (printed p. 308).

**Q5 — proved, illustrated, or proposed.** Proposed and illustrated only. The paper contains no theorem, no proof, and no derivation of an error rate. Its conservativeness statement is explicitly credited to a referee: as pointed out by a referee, this extension to unequal numbers of replications "will be a conservative test" (printed pp. 308-309). The same passage states that evaluating specific significance and prediction levels would be extremely difficult and impracticable, and warns that greatly differing replication numbers raise the probability of a significant difference inside a subset classed as homogeneous (printed p. 309). Everything from printed p. 309 heading "Numerical Example" to printed p. 310 is a worked six-treatment illustration, that is, a numerical instrument rather than a guarantee.

### 4.2 Hayter (1984), printed pp. 61-75

**Q1 — balanced procedure and its probability statement.** The model is the usual one-way fixed-effects analysis of variance with independent normal errors of common variance, treatment means based on `n_i` observations, and an unbiased variance estimate distributed independently of the means as a scaled chi-square on `ν = Σ n_i − k` degrees of freedom (printed p. 61, Section 1). Expression (1.1) on printed p. 61 states, for the equal-`n` case, that the joint probability that every difference `μ_i − μ_j` lies in the interval centred on the observed mean difference with half-width `q^{(α)}_{k,ν} S / sqrt(n)` equals exactly `1 − α`. The constant is the upper `α` point of the Studentized range distribution with parameter `k` and `ν` degrees of freedom (printed p. 61). The paper attributes this balanced T-procedure to Tukey (1953) and describes it as applying in the special case of a common sample size (printed p. 61).

**Q2 — unequal-size modification studied.** Expression (1.2) on printed p. 62 replaces `1/sqrt(n)` by `{(1/n_i + 1/n_j)/2}^{1/2}` and asserts joint coverage **at least** `1 − α` for all values of the `n_i`. The paper records that Tukey (1953) suggested this replacement and conjectured that the result is "apparently in the conservative direction" (Hayter's rendering of the manuscript, printed pp. 61-62, cited by Hayter to Tukey 1953 page 39), and that Kramer (1956) independently proposed the same modification in the slightly different context of a multiple range test procedure; expression (1.2) is accordingly named the Tukey-Kramer procedure (printed p. 62).

**Q3 — theorem proved, model, and conclusion.** The Theorem is stated on printed pp. 62-63: for independent `X_i ~ N(0, σ_i^2)` with `0 < σ_i < ∞`, and for `ξ_ij = q(σ_i^2 + σ_j^2)^{1/2}` with fixed `q > 0`, the function `F(σ_1, …, σ_k) = P{|X_i − X_j| ≤ ξ_ij; 1 ≤ i, j ≤ k}` is **strictly minimized when the `σ_i` are equal**. The paper states on printed p. 62 that, by conditioning on the variance estimate, inequality (1.2) follows from this Theorem. The proof runs from printed p. 63 to its completion on printed p. 69, organized through Lemmas 1-4 with Lemma 5 in the Appendix (printed pp. 70-75). The Remark on printed p. 63 adds two consequences: when the `σ_i` are equal `F` does not depend on their common value, and because the minimization is strict, inequality (1.2) is **strict** whenever the `n_i` are not all equal. The conclusion is therefore a general conservative coverage guarantee for the unequal-size pairwise procedure, valid for all `k` and every sample-size configuration, under the stated normal, independent, homogeneous-variance model with an independent chi-square variance estimate.

**Q4 — historical claims that are this paper's report about earlier work.** Printed p. 62 carries a compact history that must be treated as Hayter's reporting, not as evidence from the works described: Kurtz (1956), a doctoral dissertation under Tukey's supervision, proved (1.2) for `k = 3` and for nearly equal `n_i` when `k = 4`, with `k = 2` trivially an equality; Miller (1966, p. 87) advised against the procedure as inexact and lacking mathematical proof or numerical substantiation; Spjøtvoll and Stoline (1973), Dunn (1974), and Hochberg (1974a, 1975) developed alternative unequal-size procedures; Dunnett (1980) carried out an extensive simulation study indicating the conjecture's truth for all `k`; Brown (1979) proved the cases `k = 3, 4, 5`; Stoline (1981) concluded that for practical purposes the method is conservative. The rendering of Tukey's own conjectural wording, with its page-39 citation, belongs to this same reporting category.

**Q5 — extensions and problems left outside the Theorem.** Section 3, printed pp. 69-70, extends the procedure to all contrasts by way of a result the paper credits to Tukey (1953): identity (3.1) on printed p. 69 converts the pairwise constraint set into a contrast bound, and (3.2) on printed p. 70 gives simultaneous coverage at least `1 − α` for all contrasts. Printed p. 70 immediately qualifies the practical reading: the Tukey-Kramer preference over other procedures rests on shorter intervals for pairwise differences and may no longer hold once all contrasts are considered, where Scheffé's procedure may be preferred. A second extension covers designs beyond the one-way layout, where mean estimates are not independent and have a known positive-definite covariance structure; the paper records that Tukey (1953) and Kramer (1957) independently proposed the corresponding replacement, that Tukey conjectured this variant also conservative as expression (3.3), that Brown (1982) proved the case `k = 3`, and that Hochberg (1974b) obtained equality under a constant-variance-of-differences condition. That extension's general validity "remains an unsolved problem" (printed p. 70). Note also that the correlated-adjusted-means source cited here is Kramer (1957), _Biometrics_ 13, 13-18, a **different** paper from `AP-SRC-01` (reference list, printed p. 75).

### 4.3 Spjøtvoll and Stoline (1973), printed pp. 975-978

**Q1 — object of the extended T-method.** Simultaneous **estimation**, that is, simultaneous confidence intervals, not tests. The summary on printed p. 975 describes using the Studentized range to approximately simultaneously estimate all linear combinations of a set of means in an unbalanced fixed-effects model, and names the resulting methods extended Tukey procedures. Sections 2 and 3 are stated throughout as interval statements holding with a given probability (printed pp. 975-976).

**Q2 — family, assumptions, balance condition, guarantee.** The working model (2.1) on printed p. 975 takes independent estimates with the `i`-th distributed normally about its parameter with variance `a_i^2 σ^2`, the `a_i` known constants, together with an independent estimate of `σ^2` whose scaled value is chi-square on `ν` degrees of freedom; for the one-way layout of (1.1) the estimates are cell means with `a_i^2 = 1/n_i` and the variance estimate is the within-group mean square on `ν = N − k` degrees of freedom (printed p. 975). The Extended Tukey Theorem on printed p. 976 states that with probability `1 − α` all linear functions satisfy the interval statement (3.1) built on the **augmented** Studentized range point and a coefficient functional. The family is therefore all linear functions, with all contrasts and all pairwise differences as specialized consequences: expression (3.2) on printed p. 976 gives simultaneous pairwise coverage **at least** `1 − α`. Balance is not required; the construction is designed for arbitrary `a_i`. Two distinct qualifications must be kept apart: the Section 3 result is stated as **exact** when the augmented Studentized range distribution is used, whereas using the ordinary Studentized range in its place is an **approximation**, justified on printed p. 975 as differing only slightly for `α ≤ .05` and `k ≥ 3`, and adopted for the remainder of the article.

**Q3 — difference from the Tukey-Kramer modification.** Structural and explicit. The pairwise interval of (3.2) on printed p. 976 uses `max(a_i, a_j)` as the multiplier, rendered for the one-way layout in (3.3a) and (3.3b) on printed p. 976 as `max(1/sqrt(n_i), 1/sqrt(n_j))`, equivalently division by `min(sqrt(n_i), sqrt(n_j))`. The Tukey-Kramer modification instead uses the root-mean-of-reciprocals form. Because the maximum dominates that average, the extended T-method yields the wider interval whenever the two sample sizes differ. The paper does **not** cite or discuss Kramer at all (Section 6 absence record), so the comparison itself is a cross-source structural observation rather than a statement of this paper.

**Q4 — mathematical results versus computational instruments.** The mathematical content is the Extended Tukey Theorem with its short proof by rescaling to common variance (printed p. 976), the derived contrast and pairwise statements (3.1)-(3.3b) (printed p. 976), and the Section 4 uniform-preferability Result with its Cauchy-Schwarz proof (printed p. 977). The computational instruments are the ratio criterion of squared interval lengths, the unbalance measure defined as the ratio of largest to smallest sample size, and the tabulation on printed p. 978 of the comparison types for which the extended method is "uniformly preferable" to Scheffé's, computed at 95 percent and infinite degrees of freedom for a stated grid of unbalance values and numbers of means. Observations 1-4 and the two worked examples on printed pp. 977-978 read off that table and are explicitly framed as recommendations, not guarantees.

### 4.4 Dunnett (1980), printed pp. 789-795

**Q1 — six procedures, family, and model.** The family is the set of `k(k−1)/2` pairwise differences of treatment means under the one-way fixed-effects model with independent normal errors of common unknown variance (printed p. 789, Section 1). The balanced T method (1.2) is described as providing joint confidence intervals with coefficient exactly `1 − α` under common variance and equal sample sizes (printed p. 789). The six compared procedures are named in Table 3 on printed p. 792 and introduced on printed pp. 789-790: the Tukey-Kramer method (1.3); the harmonic-mean substitution; the Spjøtvoll-Stoline T′ method (1.4) using the Studentized augmented range with the maximum-reciprocal form; Hochberg's GT2 method (1.5) using the Studentized maximum modulus; the Genizi-Hochberg method (1.6), whose table is provided only for the situation where `m` or `k − m` equals 1; and Gabriel's method (1.7).

**Q2 — simulation observations versus proofs.** The paper is a **simulation study** and says so: its stated purpose is to present computer-simulation results studying the validity of the Tukey-Kramer method (printed p. 790). Phase 1 used `N = 10,000` replications for each of twelve combinations of `k ∈ {4, 6, 10}` and `ν ∈ {5, 10, 20, ∞}` at `α = .05`; phase 2 fixed `k = 6`, raised `N` to 25,000, and covered `α ∈ {.01, .05, .1, .2}` at `ν ∈ {5, 20, ∞}` (printed pp. 791-792). Normal deviates came from a named library routine with a seed-cycling check, and calibration against theoretical Studentized-range points is reported as good (printed pp. 791-792). Every conclusion in Section 3 concerning the Tukey-Kramer error rate is therefore an empirical observation. The paper is explicit that the underlying distribution of its statistic (2.1) is unknown except in the equal-`a_i` case, where it reduces to the Studentized range, and that the method's validity turns on the effect of substituting the Studentized range point (printed p. 790). Two statements in the paper are **not** its own proofs but reports of others' analytical work: that the Spjøtvoll-Stoline, GT2, and Genizi-Hochberg methods have been shown analytically to guarantee the stated coefficient for any configuration of sample sizes while giving wider limits than Tukey-Kramer (printed p. 790), and that Kurtz (1956) handled `k = 3` and Brown (1979) proved conservativeness for `k = 3, 4, 5` (printed p. 795).

**Q3 — what the paper does and does not establish.** It establishes, at simulation level, that the sampling probabilities of the Tukey-Kramer statistic exceeding the Studentized-range point decrease as the constants move away from the equal-size configuration, that this holds across `α` from .01 to .2, and that for near-equal sizes the changes fluctuate near zero and are mostly negative (printed pp. 793-794, Tables 1-2). It concludes it seems unlikely that the error rate could exceed the nominal level by more than a few percentage points, if at all (printed p. 794) — a hedged empirical judgement, not a bound. Comparatively it finds the harmonic-mean method's error rates excessive as sample sizes diverge, the Spjøtvoll-Stoline and Genizi-Hochberg methods conservative relative to Tukey-Kramer, GT2 generally conservative, and Gabriel's method conservative over a wide range but capable of excessive error rates under extreme imbalance (printed pp. 794-795, Table 3, Figures A and B). On the historical record it states that Kramer (1956) "gave no justification" for the unequal-size formula beyond intuitive appeal while mentioning a referee's suggestion of conservativeness, that Steel and Torrie noted the validity unverified, that Box, Hunter and Hunter ascribed the formula to Tukey with an incorrect reference and called it exact for equal and approximate for unequal numbers, and that Miller advised strongly against it as having "no mathematical proof or numerical substantiation" (printed pp. 789-790). It does **not** prove conservativeness for general `k`.

**Q4 — the later-proof question left open inside the 1980 paper.** Printed p. 795 states that it would be desirable, if possible, to verify the reported simulation results for the Tukey-Kramer procedure by analytical methods, and then records only the partial analytical coverage available at that time (`k = 3` via Kurtz; `k = 3, 4, 5` via Brown 1979). The general-`k` analytical question is thus explicitly open within the 1980 paper, and its recommendation to adopt the Tukey-Kramer method rests on simulation plus those partial results.

## 10. Narrow-closure decision test

**1. Can the unequal-size proposal lineage be closed from Kramer (1956)?** **Yes, closed.** The paper directly establishes the proposal, its algebraic form, its intended scope across multiple range tests, its illustrative table and example, and — decisively for the retained requirement — the fact that it proves nothing and sources its conservativeness remark to a referee (printed pp. 307-310). Independence of proposal relative to the 1953 suggestion is additionally on the record from `AP-SRC-02` p. 62 at report level.

**2. Can the general conservative guarantee be closed from Hayter (1984)?** **Yes, closed.** The Theorem (printed pp. 62-63) with its completed proof (printed pp. 63-69) yields joint coverage at least `1 − α` for all `k` and every sample-size configuration under the stated model, with strictness when sizes differ. Two scope limits travel with the closure: coverage for all contrasts is a separate Section 3 statement with its own qualification, and the non-independent-estimates variant is explicitly unresolved (printed p. 70).

**3. Can the balanced construction and guarantee be supported narrowly by a later primary formalization despite Tukey (1953) being uninspected?** **Yes, narrowly.** `AP-SRC-02` p. 61, expression (1.1), states the balanced procedure and its exact `1 − α` joint coverage in full, with the Studentized-range constant defined and the model stated. This supports a current mathematical statement with an exact pinpoint. It does **not** verify the 1953 text and must never be written as if it did.

**4. Does manuscript wording or archival fidelity remain necessary for any decision-bearing project claim, or only for historical attribution?** **Only for historical attribution.** No mathematical statement needed by the retained requirement now depends on the manuscript: the balanced guarantee and the unequal-size guarantee both have inspected later primary pinpoints. The manuscript remains necessary only for claims of the form "Tukey (1953) states/conjectures X," for the conjecture's printed-page identity, and for confirming that the 1994 printing reproduces the manuscript without semantic alteration.

**5. What is the smallest residual source requirement after this pass?** A single authenticated artifact of Tukey, "The Problem of Multiple Comparisons" (1953) — the mimeographed manuscript or the 1994 _Collected Works_ Volume VIII printing — needed solely to (a) confirm the manuscript states the balanced Studentized-range all-pairs construction and the unequal-size suggestion, (b) fix the printed-page location and wording of the conservativeness conjecture, and (c) record the archival printing's fidelity. Nothing else remains outstanding within this commission's bounded purpose.
