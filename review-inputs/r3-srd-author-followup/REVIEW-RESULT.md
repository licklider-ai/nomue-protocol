## 3. B: proofs, endpoints and graph generality

### B1. Fallback bound and exact scope

**SHOULD-FIX precision, addressed here.** Parent 5.3's first-true-error argument
is valid under these explicit assumptions: a finite, prospectively fixed order;
fixed nonnegative allocations a_i with sum at most alpha; and marginally valid
p-values, meaning `P(p_i <= c) <= c` for every true null and every deterministic
c in [0,1]. No conditioning on observed previous decisions is assumed valid.
Data-dependent weights/order would require additional justification.

Let true indices be t_1 < ... < t_s and set t_0 = 0. Before the first true error
at t_j, the previous true null was not rejected, so accumulated level cannot
cross that failed test. Therefore its tested level is at most the deterministic
budget `c_j = sum_{i=t_(j-1)+1}^{t_j} a_i`. The event E_j of a first true error
at t_j is contained in `{p_(t_j) <= c_j}`. The disjoint E_j have total probability
at most sum c_j, which is at most alpha. If s=0, the error event is empty.
Zero allocations cause no problem for this bound. Marginal validity at zero
implies that a true p-value equals zero with probability zero.

The direct first-true-null proof for fixed sequence is even smaller: all earlier
nulls are false, and any true rejection requires rejecting the first true null
at alpha. Later testing stops at the first non-rejection. This is author-side
mathematics, not an inspection of the 1995 chapter. Wiens pp.212-213 supply the
fixed-sequence definition and fallback construction/claim; his displayed fallback
proof is only for two hypotheses.

**Zero-level semantics remain an implementation choice.** The parent script uses
`level > 0 and p <= level`, whereas a literal `p <= level` also rejects a zero
p-value at zero level. For allocations (.05,0) and p=(.1,0), the former rejects
nothing and the latter rejects the second null under the fallback continuation
rule. Fixed sequence stops at the first null in this example. Consequently its
pointwise identity with fallback requires the positive-level eligibility
convention (or an appropriate exclusion of zero p-values); it is not an
unqualified identity on the closed p-value cube. An exact false-null p-value may
be zero, and machine underflow is not a valid reason to ignore this boundary.
Both conventions can satisfy the error bound, but they are different functions.
No product convention is selected here.

### B2. Closure/shortcut proof

The sufficient-direction argument in parent 7.2 survives review: for every
nonempty subset I, require predetermined nonnegative local levels with sum at
most alpha and `a_i(I) <= a_i(J)` when i is in J subset I. Define the local
Bonferroni rejection and shortcut eligibility with the **same** zero-level rule.
Select any eligible remaining index; stop only when none is eligible.

If the shortcut rejects j, each intersection containing j also contains a first
shortcut-rejected member v. At v's removal that intersection is a subset of the
remaining family. Monotonicity preserves v's local rejection inside the
intersection. Thus all intersections needed for j reject. Conversely, when
stopping with a nonempty I, its local test fails, blocking closure rejection of
every survivor. If I is empty there are no survivors. This proves equality for
all selection orders, including ties. Validity follows from the union bound
on the deterministic local levels and closed testing; no assumption about
conditional p-values is used. The positive-level version changes the stopping
statement to "no positive-level eligible member", not `p_i > a_i(I)` for every i.

Bretz pp.601-603 explicitly import the shortcut theorem from Hommel, Bretz and
Maurer [9] and give the graph-update argument. The sufficiency proof just given
is our derivation. It does not inspect [9], prove necessity, or turn an unread
primary dependency into an inspected source. The paper's regularity conditions,
finite family and well-defined local levels remain prerequisites. Its update
uses the zero branch when the reciprocal-edge product is one, avoiding a
literal division by zero. Zero ratios still need explicit selection semantics.

The supplemental diagnostic uses an independently written chain-local formula:
at surviving i, the level is the sum of base allocations after the previous
surviving index through i. It compares closure with **all eligible selection
orders** in 640 three-node allocation/p-value cases, including 0, alpha and 1,
under both consistent zero conventions, and compares the positive convention
with the parent fallback. These checks corroborate endpoints; the proof above,
not the finite grid, establishes general sufficiency. Alpha=1 and clipped
adjusted-p conventions are not promoted: an inactive zero-weight test assigned
adjusted p=1 would be called rejected at alpha=1 even when the direct positive-
level rule never rejects. Dependent use must restrict alpha<1 or define that
endpoint separately.

### B4. Finite epsilon is not the limiting procedure

**SHOULD-FIX, addressed with a bounded algebraic limit and counterexamples.**
Bretz p.596 prescribes taking epsilon to zero in levels while retaining symbolic
transition information. The parent instead chooses epsilon=1e-9, converts levels
through binary float, rounds to 1e-8, then constructs Fractions. This is rounded
numerical corroboration, not a symbolic limit or an error certificate. The
script's rejection algorithm even compares against the finite unrounded levels.

For the specific two-gatekeeper/two-secondary construction used in the parent,
an algebraic limit is available without rounding. Let gate weights a,b>0 sum
to one, secondary weights c,d>0 sum to one, initial normalized levels (a,b,0,0),
mutual gate edges 1-epsilon, gate-to-secondary edges epsilon(c,d), and mutual
secondary edges one. For nonempty surviving secondary set S, define
`q_s = w_s / sum_{t in S} w_t`. The exact finite-epsilon local weights are:

| Surviving gatekeepers                 | Surviving local weights                           |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Both                                  | a,b on the gates; zero on secondaries             |
| Only i; deleted gate j has weight w_j | `1-w_j*epsilon` on i; `w_j*epsilon*q_s` on s in S |
| Neither                               | q_s on s in S                                     |

If S is empty, omit its terms; leaked mass is lost. The one-gate row follows
one removal. Its outgoing secondary transition becomes
`epsilon*(2-epsilon)*q_s / (1-(1-epsilon)^2) = q_s`.
Removing the last gate therefore sends
`[1-w_j*epsilon]+w_j*epsilon = 1` to S. Eliminating either secondary first simply
renormalizes (c,d) to q. This covers all 15 nonempty subsets, including gate-only
ones. Taking epsilon to zero gives exactly Algorithm 2's local weights for this
strictly positive 2+2 construction. This is an author-side algebraic derivation,
not a general-family theorem. The diagnostic checks all 15 finite-epsilon
formulas exactly at the parent's rational epsilon as an algebra check.

Two exact examples explain why substituting finite epsilon is unsafe even here.
Use alpha=.05, (a,b,c,d)=(.9,.1,.5,.5), epsilon=1e-9:

- For p=(.09,.004,1e-12,1), H2 rejects. H1's level is
  `.05-.005*epsilon`, so H1 survives; H3 receives `2.5e-12` and rejects.
  The intended serial rule blocks H3 while H1 survives. The finite script
  rejects H2 and H3; this is an explicit decision difference.
- For p=(.05,.004,1,1), H2 rejects but the finite level at H1 is strictly
  below .05, so H1 survives for **every** positive epsilon. The limiting
  procedure rejects H1 at equality. These p-values are fixed independently of
  epsilon. Convergence of levels does not imply convergence of boundary decisions.

The Figure 9/10/12 finite examples in the parent remain historical numerical
observations only; no symbolic proof of every improved or multi-family epsilon
procedure is supplied. The 2+2 algebra above does not certify those extensions.

**C2, SHOULD-FIX: narrow "error control unaffected" to Bonferroni.** At a fixed
intersection with predetermined nonnegative local weights v summing to at most
one, the union of events `p_i <= alpha*v_i` for positive v has probability at
most alpha. Raw subunit-mass and normalized unit-mass weights both meet this
condition, under marginal validity and without dependence assumptions. Closure
then gives strong FWER. This proves validity, not equality of adjusted values,
not Condition 1, and not a Simes result.

For the weighted-Simes expression inspected on p.2393, sorting raw p-values gives
`min_l p_(l)/(v_(1)+...+v_(l))`. If the raw mass is s>0, normalization multiplies
this uncapped expression by s; the relationship is applied before clipping at
one. A valid normalized local test would imply conservatism of the raw version
because s<=1. **The needed validity premise is not established by the weight-sum
argument.** The source attributes positive-regression-dependence validity to
Kling and Benjamini's unpublished 2002 manuscript, not inspected here. No proof
under those conditions is claimed, and no blanket failure under them is alleged.

A small exact counterexample shows why marginal validity alone is inadequate.
For alpha=.05, let (p1,p2) have masses .025 at each of (.025,1), (1,.025), and
(.05,.05), and mass .925 at (1,1). Both marginals are super-uniform: at their
only nontrivial support points .025 and .05 the cumulative probabilities are
.025 and .05. With equal weights, Simes rejects on the first three atoms, giving
local error .075; Bonferroni rejects only the first two, giving .05. This example
asserts no positive-regression-dependence property. It disproves only the attempted
extension from arbitrary dependence/marginal validity to Simes. Simes or resampling
promotion still requires its own primary-source work; no such extension is needed
for this bounded Bonferroni characterization.
